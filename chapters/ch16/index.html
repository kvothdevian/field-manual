Chapter 16 · Your daily agentic workflow

facts checked as of 2026-08-26 · this chapter introduces no new tools; it compiles fifteen chapters of them into one cadence

After this chapter you own a personal operating rhythm — four moves per session, three per week, three at every project kickoff — the habits that separate people who ship with agents from people who demo with them.

Harnesses rewrite themselves monthly. Models leap every quarter. None of that is where the game is won or lost. Watch someone demo with an agent and then watch someone ship with one: the difference is rarely talent or budget — it is a handful of boring, repeated moves, done every single time, especially when nothing seemed to go wrong last time. This chapter folds the whole book into a calendar. Nothing in it is new theory; every move cites the chapter that earned it.

One rhythm, three scales

The same skeleton fits a student, a professional, and a tinkerer. The magnitudes differ; the moves do not.

The student, shipping a first real project

She has one evening and one feature. Before opening the harness she writes the finish line — "these three tests pass, no other files touched" — because chapter 01 taught her that a loop without a detectable goal stops at the context wall, not at done. One session per concern. When the build fails she pastes the exact stack trace instead of describing it from memory. She reads every diff before accepting; twice this week the plausible patch was wrong, and reading caught it. On Friday she deletes two stale rules from her five-line AGENTS.md, reruns her test command end-to-end, and logs one thing the agent got worse at. Total ritual overhead: fifteen minutes a week.

The developer, adopting agents at work

His blast radius is bigger, so the same moves get witnesses. Finish lines are written where a teammate can see them. Every concern gets its own worktree and branch, because two agents sharing one checkout trample each other and chapter 04's --worktree isolation exists precisely for this. Diffs get reviewed like any human pull request — no sentimental discount. His weekly sweep includes the CI gates, not just dependencies, and his Friday retro note goes to the team channel, where one honest line about a regression saves everyone else a bad afternoon.

The tinkerer, running local models

His hardware sets the ceiling: a 64-thousand-token context is not generous, so chapter 05's economy rules stop being optional. Smaller finish lines — one test, one file — because every wasted pass is a larger fraction of the window. He pastes exact errors all the same; slow hardware punishes wandering loops harder than fast ones. Weekly grooming matters most for him: he copies configurations between machines, and chapter 04's rot compounds with every copy. Same rhythm, tighter circles.

Notice what all three share: nothing heroic. No twelve-step morning routine, no dashboard. Everything below is those ten moves, taught.

Four moves a day, three a week, three at kickoff.

§2

The daily four

One: state the finish line before the session. One falsifiable sentence the loop can detect — tests passing, a table rendering, an exit code hitting zero. Chapter 01 built this: clear success criteria are the highest-leverage habit in agentic work, and vague goals are how loops wander. Write it where you can see it when the session gets tempting.

Two: review the diff before you accept. Run the proof, read the patch, then accept — the verify step of the outer loop, and the first thing everyone starts skipping. It gets its own honesty section below.

Three: one worktree per concern. Five features in one endless conversation is chapter 01's kitchen-sink session: context fills, quality sags, the agent contradicts decisions it made an hour ago, and the diff can't be untangled afterward. The antidote is physical, not willpower — a separate branch and checkout per concern, trivial to throw away. Restarting clean usually beats rescuing a bloated thread.

Four: paste exact errors. Verbatim output, not a paraphrase, not "it doesn't work." Chapter 10's reproduce-first discipline starts with the literal text; paraphrased symptoms send the loop chasing ghosts, and ghost-chasing is where thrash lives.

Convergence risk: the drift is the default

Here is the uncomfortable row this book refuses to bury. In May 2026, Simon Willison — whose definitional essays shaped this book's vocabulary — published a self-critical reflection conceding that even careful practitioners gradually stop reviewing every line as tools get smoother and trust accumulates. The trend line bends toward acceptance without inspection, and nothing in the tooling pushes accountability back; it simply stays yours anyway. The daily diff review is not a talisman that makes you correct — it is a tripwire that makes drift visible while the diff is still small enough to read. Skip it once and nothing happens, which is exactly the trap.

§3

The weekly three

One: groom the memory files. Open your AGENTS.md (and any auto-memory index) with one question: which rules are stale? Kill anything you cannot explain the reason for. Chapter 04's config-rot warning is blunt: a stale rule does not merely stop helping, it misleads — last year's "always use library X" turns every fresh session into a fight against your own file. Date rules as you add them; documented load caps on memory indexes are a second argument for short files, since overflow silently goes unread.

Two: sweep dependencies and gates. Bump the pins, rerun the verifier end-to-end, read the results. Chapter 12's premise is that bloat and drift arrive on their own; the sweep is the scheduled resistance. Much of it automates — GitHub's Dependabot, for instance, updates pinned GitHub Actions from a config file — but automation only nominates the change. Reading gate output stays a human chore, on purpose.

Three: hold a retro — one thing the agent got worse at. Not a mood, a log entry. Chapter 07's hardest receipt applies: in METR's randomized trial, experienced developers believed they were 20% faster while measuring 19% slower. Feelings about your tools are not data. You need no daily stopwatch — but once a week write down one observed regression, and occasionally measure something real: commit history, a timed task, a boring spreadsheet. Update on evidence, not vibes.

§4

The kickoff three

Every new project gets three moves before any feature work:

  • Seed the AGENTS.md — five lines. What this is, the exact test command, one dated rule. Chapter 04's promotion rule governs growth: correct the agent twice about the same thing, and the correction enters the file. Five lines today beats a constitution never.
  • Branch first. New concern, new branch, before the briefing. Chapter 11's understand-before-edit workflow quietly assumes the work lands somewhere disposable; work committed straight to main is work you cannot cleanly throw away.
  • Pin the verifier. Choose, on day one, the one command that proves done, and write it into the memory file. Chapter 06's red-green discipline needs a test that can fail; with no pinned verifier, no session can stop honestly.
tip

End every session by leaving a note to your next session — three lines: where you stopped, what's next, any trap you just discovered. It is the cheapest external memory you own. Compaction summarizes transcripts well (chapter 05), but a hand-written handoff survives every mechanism and turns tomorrow's cold start into a warm one.

§5

Simplest pattern applies to rituals too

Do not install ten habits on Monday; you will abandon nine by Thursday and distrust the tenth. Chapter 06's doctrine — simplest thing that could possibly work, complexity added only when measurements demand it — applies to your own routine. Grow the rhythm on evidence, with the same promotion rule as memory files: adopt a ritual the moment the same mistake costs you twice. Until then, the daily four carry most of the weight.

§6

The gauntlet, weekly, on your own work

Chapter 06 introduced this book's house pattern with honest labeling, kept here: the gauntlet has no quantitative validation — no study benchmarks it. Its nearest studied anchors are the evaluator-optimizer pattern, Reflexion's reflection loop, and judge alignment. As a weekly self-review ritual it works like this: pick one piece of your own output, fetch the bar (for us, Anthropic's best-practices page), and have a fresh-context agent compare your piece against that bar blind — binary verdict, single biggest gap. The gap becomes Monday's finish line. That is how every chapter of this book was finished.

§7

Where this landed in our builds

No demonstration section this time — nothing left to demonstrate. So we will say it plainly instead: both companion builds ran on exactly this cadence while the book was being written. agent-cost-compass and ncert-quiz-forge opened each milestone with a stated finish line from their build specs, worked one concern per branch behind review gates, and ended sessions with handoff notes. The proof it pays: during a build review, a subtly wrong pricing claim about DeepSeek's off-peak rates was caught by this loop — diff read, live source re-checked, ledger corrected. The cadence in this chapter is not advice we wrote for you. It is how the book itself got shipped.

    §10

    Try it yourself

    1. Exact-click checkpoint. In the builder above, select the daily four and the weekly three, read every graded line, then press print cheat-sheet card. Tape the card where you start sessions. Those seven moves cost roughly ninety minutes a week — cheaper than one rescued kitchen-sink session.
    2. Break it on purpose. Uncheck review diffs before accepting and read what the grader does. Then run one real session where you skip the diff review deliberately, and afterwards inspect git diff for what slipped. Once is usually enough to make the ritual permanent.
    3. Ship-sized task. Run the full rhythm for one week on your own project — finish lines, diff reviews, worktrees, Friday grooming and retro. Keep the retro log; it becomes your first quarterly prune's agenda, and the raw material for your own gauntlet.
    §11

    Self-check

    What separates the daily four from the weekly three?

    Timescale and purpose. The daily four are per-session loop hygiene — goal, verification, isolation, evidence quality. The weekly three are maintenance that prevents silent decay: config rot, dependency drift, and unexamined assumptions about the tools themselves.

    You skip memory-file grooming for a month. What specifically breaks?

    Nothing loud — that is the danger. Stale rules stay loaded every session and actively steer it wrong: old commands, dead conventions, contradicted decisions. You experience it as "the agent got worse," when the file is fighting the present. Date rules; delete what you cannot explain.

    When should you break your own rules?

    Deliberately, on evidence, in writing. The retro and quarterly prune exist so measurement can override ritual — if the log shows a move catches nothing, retire it and note why. What you must not do is break rules silently mid-session because the diff looked long. Drift is the default; the ritual is the tripwire.

    §12

    Beyond this build

    Teams. Personal rhythms harden into team conventions: the finish line becomes the ticket's acceptance criteria, the diff review becomes the PR gate, the weekly sweep becomes a rota. Scale the ceremony to the blast radius; let whoever owns the bar own the ritual.

    Onboarding others. Hand newcomers the cheat-sheet card, not the vibes. Their first-week deliverable is the kickoff three on their own machine: seeded AGENTS.md, branch-first habit, pinned verifier. Someone who can run those three can be trusted with the daily four unsupervised.

    When to break your own rules. The rhythm serves shipping, never the reverse. Deadlines legitimately compress rituals — but compression is a decision you make consciously and write down, not drift you notice later. If a rule keeps losing to reality, change it at the prune; if it keeps saving you, promote it. Observe, prune, promote — that loop is this entire book in one sentence.

    Where you go from here

    The chapters are done; the practice starts now. Three doors:

    The appendices. The contents page leads to the reference shelf: a per-harness tool cheat sheet, the glossary, and the coverage matrix that guarantees what this book promised to teach, it taught.

    The source ledger. Every claim in all sixteen chapters traces to a tiered, dated row in the book's source ledger — the same discipline chapter 07 taught you to demand of anyone else's benchmarks. Re-verify anything; URLs age and so do we.

Point them at your next project. Then leave yourself a note about what to build next — your next session is already grateful.
