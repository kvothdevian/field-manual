Chapter 13 · Shipping for $0

Promise: after this chapter you can put your project on a real public URL today — today, not someday — at literally zero cost, and name the exact wall your traffic will hit first on whichever host you pick.

facts checked as of 2026-08-26 · every hosting number below carries its own check date — they expire quietly

The whole ritual, before any vendor

Shipping sounds like infrastructure. It is three boring steps, and every free host on earth is just a different brand of the same three:

  1. Produce a static output. A folder of HTML, CSS, and JS that any web server could serve as-is. Plain hand-written pages qualify; so does whatever build step your generator runs. If double-clicking index.html shows your site, step one is done.
  2. Put that folder in a git repository pushed to a public forge. The repo is the delivery mechanism — hosts watch it, not your laptop.
  3. Connect a host to the repo, once. You click through an import screen, accept defaults, and the host builds the folder and hands you a URL on its own domain. From then on, deploying is one command: push. The host notices, rebuilds, and the public URL updates.

That last sentence is the entire payoff: setup is an afternoon; afterwards, shipping costs one command forever. Most hosts also offer a small command-line uploader for folder-in-hand deploys — a second flavor of the same one-command endgame. Choose by feel; the ritual transfers.

But free hosting is never limit-free, and Chapter 03 taught the durable skill already: read the published wall before you build on it. Hosting walls come in two species, and confusing them is the classic beginner injury. Numeric walls are metered — builds per month, gigabytes served — and they trip silently mid-month. Legal walls are contractual — clauses in a terms page that no dashboard will ever warn you about. One is arithmetic. The other is law. Both can take your site down.

Three lessons the finder should leave behind, none of them vendor-specific. First, walls differ in kind, not just height: Cloudflare and Netlify ration your pushes, Vercel rations both pushes and readers, and GitHub Pages publishes only soft, guidance-grade caps — its hardest rationing is still what you may legally be. Second, the same traffic costs different walls on different hosts: 300,000 launch-week visitors sail through on two of these plans and trip Vercel's transfer line at ordinary page weights. Third, a missing number is not a big number. Where our sources pin no traffic cap, the finder says so plainly — unpinned means unverified, and unverified is exactly when you go read the source yourself.

Preview deploys: review the real thing, not a screenshot

The single best habit free hosting buys you costs nothing on every host above: every branch gets its own URL before anyone merges it. Push a fix to a branch and the host quietly builds it at a private address — your reviewer opens the link and clicks the actual change, on an actual phone, before it touches the main site. Cloudflare's documentation formalizes exactly this pattern under the name preview deployments; the other git-connected hosts behave the same way, and Netlify's pricing page goes further, listing deploy previews as unlimited even on the credit-starved free tier. The workflow is host-generic: branch → automatic preview URL → human review → merge → production rebuild. It converts "trust me, it works" into "here is the link," and it is worth choosing a host for all by itself.

One host's wall is made of law

Now read the boundary honestly, because it is the one beginners actually hit. GitHub's terms for additional products — effective April 27, 2026 — do not allow GitHub Pages to be used as a free hosting service for a commercial business, e-commerce site, or SaaS product. That clause is not folklore and not a rumor from a forum: it is the contract you clicked. Personal sites, portfolios, project demos, documentation, community pages — all comfortably inside. The terms even narrow the money question themselves: donation buttons and crowdfunding links are explicitly permitted on Pages, so this bar was never "any money" — it targets sites that exist to run a commercial business. The moment the site is the storefront of a business, you are outside the written terms even though every dial still reads zero. Enforcement is sporadic and unpredictable, which is precisely why "they never enforce it" is not a plan. The deeper lesson generalizes: some walls are legal, not numeric, and no monitoring tool you will ever install watches that kind.

Deploy = one command, after setup

This book practices what it preaches on its own timeline: local git only through Phase 4, then a single push to GitHub and Pages flips on — a decision recorded, dated, and revisitable. Your projects deserve the same ceremony, generically. After the one-time connection ritual (produce static output → commit → import repo into your chosen host → accept defaults), deployment collapses to git push. Everything downstream — build, preview, promotion — is the host's problem. Resist wiring custom deploy scripts before your tenth push; the plain ritual is the skill, and it transfers unchanged between all four hosts above.

Let the agent write the release notes

Shipping is not only uploading; it is telling humans what changed. This is a chore agents are genuinely good at: hand one your merged commits or a tag diff and ask for notes in your house style — what changed, who noticed, what to do about it. The pattern is a recurring job, and Chapter 12's pipeline recipes (docs-sync, triage, scheduled maintenance) are the same shape wearing different clothes; run release-notes on a schedule or on every tag and the habit compounds. One honesty rule from Chapter 06 applies verbatim: the agent drafts, you verify against the actual diff, then it ships. Notes nobody vetted are rumors with formatting.

Where this lands in your build

Both threaded builds already made this exact decision, in writing, and their reasoning is the template for yours. The agent-cost-compass targets GitHub Pages: it is free static hosting co-located with the GitHub Actions cron that feeds the compass weekly diffs — one platform hosts the computation and the result. The ncert-quiz-forge chose Cloudflare Pages instead, for reasons its BUILD.md records verbatim: Indian edge POPs near its actual students, bandwidth headroom for exam-season spikes, _headers control, and preview deploys — with deployment deliberately gated until the ship milestone so $0 stays intact meanwhile. Neither choice is universally right; both are written down with dates. Do likewise: when you pick, record host, caps, and the date you checked them in your own BUILD.md.

tip

Ship the ugly version this week. A live, embarrassing URL beats a beautiful localhost every time — real hosting surfaces real problems (paths, caching, mobile widths) that no local preview reproduces, and every future chapter assumes you have a URL to point at. Setup is an afternoon; polish can ride later on the same ritual.

warning

Free-tier limits move silently. Vendors reprice plans without banners on your dashboard, and Chapter 03 already watched one beloved free door close entirely. Every number in this chapter carries its check date; stamp your host's limits date in your own notes the day you read them, and re-stamp quarterly. The finder teaches the method, not eternal truths.

Try it yourself — ship something today

  1. Exact-click checkpoint. Create a new public repo on your forge containing one index.html that says "hello, public world" (web-upload works — typing in a browser is enough). Open your chosen host's dashboard, run its import/connect flow against the repo, accept every default, and wait for the build badge. Open the URL it hands you. That URL is yours; send it to one human.
  2. Break it on purpose. In the wall finder, set the viral-month profile, predict which wall trips first before reading the verdict, then check. Switch hosts and find the one that shrugs that same load off. Finally flip commercial-use on GitHub Pages and watch a wall fire that no dial can measure.
  3. Ship-sized task. Point the ritual at your actual project tonight. Then open your BUILD.md and add one line: host chosen, the caps you checked, and today's date beside each — your personal restamping reminder, exactly as the builds did.

Self-check

You push to production 2,000 times in a month. What breaks on Cloudflare versus Netlify?

Cloudflare: the published 500-builds/month cap — pushes 501 onward stall until the month turns. Netlify: the credit wallet — 300 credits at 15 per production deploy buys 20, so death arrives at deploy 21, long before 2,000. Same behavior, wildly different budgets; the finder shows both.

Why is GitHub Pages' most important wall absent from every dial?

Because it is contractual, not metered: the additional-products terms (eff. 2026-04-27) bar commercial-business/e-commerce/SaaS hosting, and a separate clause reserves suspension rights against disproportionate burden. Legal walls have no dashboard counter — you learn of them from a terms page, ideally before shipping, not an outage email after.

Name the moment paid hosting genuinely earns itself.

When a published wall measurably contacts real usage — sustained traffic over a transfer cap, a build cadence over the monthly budget, a business model the free terms forbid — and re-architecting around the wall costs more than the invoice. Pay for the wall you actually hit, never the one you imagine.

Beyond this build

Custom domains. The *.host.example URL is training wheels; a domain you own outlives every host. Our pinned rows confirm the path: GitHub Pages documents custom domains, and Netlify's free tier includes custom domains with SSL. The other hosts document domain support on the same pages as their limits — verify there, since this book cites only what its ledger pins. Owning the name means changing hosts is a settings screen, not a rebranding.

Analytics-lite. Once real people arrive you will want to count them — resist heavyweight trackers. A few lines measuring page loads, or your host's own traffic figures, answer the only question that matters at this stage: is anyone here? Anything richer deserves the privacy scrutiny Chapter 14 teaches before you embed it.

Where it breaks down. Static hosting serves files; it does not run your backend, hold secrets, or sign transactions. The moment a project needs a database, authenticated APIs, or scheduled heavy jobs, you are composing services again — still often $0 at hobby scale, but now several walls from several vendors, each needing its own stamped date. And when a free tier's silence about a limit is doing load-bearing work in your plan, that is not architecture; that is hope with a deadline.
