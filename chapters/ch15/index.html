Price it before you touch it

Briefing · 5 min · read the meter, cap the month, skip the shortcut

i.Your bill has three dials and a hidden price

Every agent session spends four things, and only three appear on a dashboard. The burst dial caps how fast you may ask: requests per minute, the bucket behind a "slow down" 429. The throughput dial caps how much you may move: input and output tokens per minute, the pipe behind a "send less" 429. The budget dial caps what you may spend: the monthly ceiling where the API stops answering until the first of next month. The hidden price is your data: which doors train on prompts, which let you opt out, and which never say — because silence about training is itself a term you accept.

The ritual is four numbers plus a date: RPM, input tokens per minute, output tokens per minute, and your cap — written down the day you read them. Anthropic's rate-limits page is the worked example, re-verified live 2026-09-04: Start-tier Opus-class traffic gets 1,000 requests per minute, 2,000,000 input tokens per minute, 400,000 output tokens per minute, under a $500 monthly cap, and cached input reads do not count toward the input limit on current models. When a 429 names the input-token limit, the throughput dial tripped — fix it with caching or smaller context, never blind retry.

Distrust every meter you did not read yourself. Subscription doors pool allowances across products — Codex draws from one shared pool with the rest of the plan, and the remaining balance lives behind the plan's own status screen, not in any chapter table. Token-credit doors convert everything to one opaque unit whose burn depends on model, context, and reasoning depth. Vendor averages are order-of-magnitude weather, not your forecast. The ritual reads your door, today, in writing.

Some pages print the trade; some print nothing, and the nothing is citable. OpenRouter's privacy page states the deal: per-provider training flags, separate opt-outs for paid and free models, request-level filtering — opting out may exclude the cheapest doors, and that exclusion is the price tag. The z.ai usage policy, fetched live 2026-09-04, says nothing about input training: no opt-out to point at, no retention promise to quote. That silence is not permission. Gemini and Mistral training claims stay third-party leads, warning-fenced, never fact.

Grey-zone arithmetic scores two axes plus a forecast. Stability: does the shortcut survive the next provider change, or is it reverse-engineered surface that dies without notice? Legality: what do the written terms actually prohibit — credential sharing, limit circumvention, reselling access? Then the death-forecast: how long until this stops working, and what breaks with it. Relays that launder one subscription into another tool fail both axes in writing, and uptime proves nothing. This chapter's exhibit is triaged on paper, never executed. Table numbers expire; the recheck date is the artifact. The $0 doors from ch03 remain the on-ramp.

ii.Three dials and a side channel · which dial tripped my 429?

A rate-limit error is a diagnosis, not a mood. Requests per minute is the burst bucket: it fills with each call and drains steadily. Input and output tokens per minute form the throughput pipe: long contexts trip it even at a polite request rate. The monthly spend cap is the budget ceiling: past it, requests stop with a limit error and no retry header. Read which limit the error names before touching anything — each dial has exactly one fix, and the wrong fix burns the session twice.

The side channel needs its own reading because it never returns an error code. A door that trains on your prompts charges you in future-model weights. OpenRouter answers both questions in writing — per-provider flags, separate paid and free opt-outs — so the trade is priced. The z.ai policy answers neither, so the trade is unpriced, which is worse than expensive. The chapter rule: an unpriced trade is never assumed free; it is filed as unknown, dated, and routed around for anything you would not publish.

iii.The four-number ritual · RPM, in, out, cap, date

Open the door's own limits page and write five tokens on paper: requests per minute, input tokens per minute, output tokens per minute, the spend cap, and today's date. The date is the load-bearing field — rate cards change, and August numbers are fertilizer, not facts. Do this for each door you actually use, monthly. The OpenRouter constants are the worked example, re-verified live 2026-09-04 from the page source: free-variant models allow 20 requests per minute always, 50 requests per day until the account has bought at least $10 of credits, then 1,000 per day — and making extra accounts or keys does not move the limit. A 402 means money; a 429 means rate; the fix follows the code.

v.Grey-zone arithmetic · stability × legality + death-forecast

Score every shortcut on two axes before pricing it. Stability: is this documented surface or reverse-engineered surface — will it survive the next provider change, or does it break without notice? Legality: what do the written terms prohibit, in quoted words? Then add the death-forecast: how long until it stops working, and what breaks with it. Risk is probability times consequence on each axis, and the canonical mistake is scoring only the saving.

The clauses behind the pinned card, each fetched live 2026-09-04. Anthropic's consumer terms prohibit sharing login, API key, or account credentials with anyone else. OpenAI's 2026 terms add three walls: no sharing account credentials, no circumventing rate limits or protective measures, no using output to develop competing models. GitHub's additional-products terms, effective 2026-04-27, draw the stacking boundary: Actions may not carry disproportionate burden or serve as a standalone commercial service, and Pages may not host commercial SaaS (donation buttons and crowdfunding links are explicitly permitted). The relay exhibit itself — a 4.1k-star reverse proxy — carries its own warning label: not supported by the vendor, use at your own risk, with an abuse-detection notice. Uptime is not permission.

Where this breaks down: numbers expire, so the recheck date is the artifact — a price without one is a rumor with formatting. Residency risk (one guide flags China-hosted serving plus Entity List churn for the z.ai surface, with open-weights self-hosting as the clean path) stays a dated third-party lead: good questions for your compliance lead, never conclusions. Copyright's hub anchors the authorship side: Part 2 on copyrightability published 2025-01-29, Part 3 on training still pre-publication — the training-law question is unsettled at the source, which is why the chapter prices data trades conservatively.
