Promise: after this chapter you can stress-test any AI-productivity claim in about ten minutes — using the same three-tier system every receipt in this book was graded by.

Hype arrives pre-packaged: a number, a logo, a chart going up and to the right. This chapter is the ten-minute routine for opening the package before you repeat what is inside — the book's own vetting discipline, turned around and handed to you. You will autopsy three real claims, dissect one famous vendor study, watch a benchmark get gamed three ways, follow one claim from launch video to public walk-back, and meet the machine that grades this book's own sources.

Read a claim like a coroner

Most AI claims die from one of five wounds, and you can check for all five faster than you can read the press release:

  • No control group? Faster than what? A credible number needs a comparison condition — same people, same tasks, randomly assigned with or without the tool. Without one, "55% faster" means "faster than nothing, which we didn't measure."
  • Who measured? Participants' feelings are not instruments; the gap between perceived and clocked speed is the best-documented effect in this field. Stopwatch, logs, or vibes?
  • Funding disclosure? Whoever pays the piper picks the spreadsheet. Funding is not automatically disqualifying — undisclosed funding is.
  • Contamination check? If a benchmark's problems sat on the public internet before a model's training cutoff, a high score may measure memory. Ask when test items were created versus when training stopped.
  • Scope? A result holds in its studied population and nowhere else until proven otherwise. "Experienced open-source maintainers on 2025 tools" is not "everyone."

The professional skeptics Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor built standing checklists on exactly these questions at AI Snake Oil; the heuristics above deliberately rhyme with theirs. And when you chase sources, grade them before you trust them: T1 official docs, specs, repos, or established practitioners, verified before citing · T2 vendor blogs and aggregators, presumed marketing · T3 forums and social posts, leads only, never cited as fact without corroboration.

The METR study, both acts

You met this result in Chapter 01 as a warning about trusting your sense of speed. Here it is whole, because the second act matters more than the first. In July 2025 the independent nonprofit METR ran a randomized controlled trial: experienced open-source developers, real issues in repos they knew well, early-2025 agentic tools. Participants predicted a 24% speedup; afterwards they believed they had achieved about 20%. Measured reality: the AI group took roughly 19% longer. Data and code were public.

Act two: in February 2026, METR's researchers publicly walked back confidence in their own method. Selection effects had crept in — skilled developers increasingly refused to be randomized into a no-AI arm — and follow-up work suggests late-2025 tools likely help more than the trial could show. Same institution, same public trail, updated conclusion.

The lesson is not "studies flip, ignore them." A source that publishes its own doubt, with dates and data attached, is worth more than one that has never blinked. Intellectual honesty is a behavior you can watch for.

Anatomy of a vendor study

Now the counter-example this book keeps returning to. In December 2022, GitHub published research quantifying Copilot's impact: developers completed a task 55% faster. Run the checklist. Who measured? The participants themselves, timing their own work — the exact faculty METR later showed is unreliable. Control group? None. Funding? The seller studied its own product. Sample? Ninety-five people in a designed experiment. Each tell fires. Fair in both directions: this does not prove Copilot fails to help — it proves the 55% figure, as stated, was never established. The contrast with METR's design is the entire lesson: same question, opposite rigor.

How benchmarks get gamed

Benchmark scores look like measurements and behave like marketing. Three documented failure modes:

Gaming by leakage. The academic SWE-Bench+ audit re-examined the popular SWE-bench coding benchmark and found roughly a third of "resolved" instances leaked solution information and about a quarter passed weak tests; when the suite was tightened, SWE-Agent+GPT-4's score collapsed from 12.47% to 4.58%, and issues published after models' training cutoffs scored lower still. "Resolved" was doing marketing work.

Gaming by contamination. LiveCodeBench attacked the problem structurally: it continuously harvests coding problems published after model release dates and measures performance on those alone. On static benchmarks whose problems predate training, models show large overfitting drops — proof that familiarity inflates familiar test sets.

Gaming by governance. Epoch AI ran FrontierMath, a famously hard math benchmark, until January 2025 brought double news: OpenAI had quietly funded the benchmark and held pre-release access to problems and solutions before o3's record-setting run. Epoch itself called the nondisclosure a failure, said so plainly, and built a holdout set. TechCrunch covered it, quoting named actors. Notice what fixed it: disclosure, dates, and a locked drawer of unseen problems.

The Devin arc: death of a claim in public

In March 2024, the startup Cognition launched Devin, "the first AI software engineer," with videos showing it completing real jobs on Upwork. Watch what happened next. Within a month, The Pragmatic Engineer documented that the framing had been walked back — the showcased jobs were not completed end-to-end as advertised. Independently, a YouTube creator posted a frame-by-frame autopsy of the launch demo: hallucinated fixes, unmet requirements, and a human replicating the demo task in 36 minutes against Devin's six-hour-plus run.

Here is the sourcing pattern worth stealing. The video alone is T3 — a lead, never proof; anyone can edit anything. It earns its place only because a T1 practitioner journalist independently documented the same failures. Call it the T3-with-T1-corroboration pattern: rumor finds the trail, reporting walks it. When you hear an extraordinary demo claim, search for exactly this pairing before believing or repeating it.

Memorization is not skill

A December 2025 University of Waterloo-group preprint ran a clever test: give models file-localization tasks drawn from SWE-Bench-Verified alongside equivalent tasks from fresh, comparable benchmarks. Claude models located the SWE-bench files 3–6× better — on a benchmark whose contents have been public for years. That is recall, not skill, and it explains why leaderboard scores feel better than the same models on your unfamiliar codebase. One flag, prominently: this is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. The tier system grades process — clever design, public artifacts, flagged status.

warning

Benchmarks are marketing until proven otherwise. Every gaming mode above was discovered by outsiders asking these questions — leakage, contamination, governance — months after the celebration graphs shipped.

Autopsy scorecard

The ten-minute transferable checklist

  • Follow funding before findings — who paid for the number, and was that disclosed?
  • Who measured? Participants' feelings, the seller's staff, or an independent party with clocks and logs?
  • Control group? Compared against what — matched people, matched tasks, random assignment?
  • Contamination check? Do the test problems predate the model's training cutoff?
  • Scope? Does the studied population match what the headline implies for you?
  • Tier check? Is there a T1 anchor — and is any T3 lead paired with real corroboration?

Six questions, one verdict vocabulary — assigned before you hit share.

The machine this book runs on

Everything above is a technique. Here it is running as infrastructure. Before any chapter of this book was drafted, a research wave built a source ledger: six parallel research agents produced roughly 160 rows covering all sixteen chapters and Appendix A, each row recording the URL, the exact claim it supports, its tier, an as-of date, and whether it was fetched live. Chapters may only cite ledger rows. T1 anchors carry the load; T2 vendor material is flagged as marketing inline; T3 appears only as dated leads paired with warnings or stronger corroboration — exactly like the Devin video above.

Does the machine catch errors — including its own? One receipt proves it. The ledger's DeepSeek pricing row originally described its API rates in a way that implied the standard rate was simply halved off-peak. During Build 1 review, the protocol demanded a live re-fetch of the pricing page; the live numbers showed the quoted figures were the off-peak rates, full stop — the original wording was wrong. The correction went into the ledger, dated, that same day. Nobody scolded anybody; verification-before-citation found its own mistake and left a paper trail.

Which brings the thread home. Every receipt you have clicked in Chapters 01 through 04 was graded by this machine before you ever saw its badge, and Appendix B will eventually print the ledger raw. The badges were never decoration; they were verdicts.

tip

Follow funding before findings. It is the fastest question to ask and the one most often answered honestly — Epoch disclosed its OpenAI funding failure in its own voice, which is exactly why Epoch is still citable. The dangerous sources are not the conflicted ones; they are the quietly conflicted ones.

Where this lands in your build

Your builds inherit the ledger discipline directly. When the agent-cost-compass quotes a price, when a chapter quotes a limit, the number arrives with a URL, a tier, and a date — because every number in a project someone else will verify is a claim awaiting autopsy. Adopt the same shape for anything your agents produce that asserts facts: claim, source, tier, date. It costs minutes and converts "the agent said so" into something you can defend.

Try it yourself

  1. Exact-click checkpoint. Open the GitHub blog post on Copilot's impact and find two things in under three minutes: how many developers, and who recorded the task times. Then open METR's study page and find its sample description and public-data link. Write the two designs side by side, one line each. You have just done a real autopsy.
  2. Break it on purpose. Pick any model-launch headline from this week. Before touching a receipt, run the six-question checklist and write your predicted verdict — supports, refutes, or irrelevant. Only then check the sources; the gap between guess and evidence is your calibration score.
  3. Ship-sized task. Create a LEDGER.md in your own project with the ledger's columns — URL, claim, tier, as-of, fetched? — and enter the next three facts your agent asserts about the outside world. Rule: no claim may appear in prose until it appears in the table.

Self-check

Why does the Copilot 55% study fail as evidence even if everyone in it was honest?

Honesty is not validity. The design lacks a control group, relies on self-timed measurements (the faculty METR showed drifts tens of points from reality), and was funded and run by the product's seller. Together those mean the number is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The Devin debunk is a YouTube video — why is it in this book at all?

As a T3 lead, never as standalone proof. It qualifies under the T3-with-T1-corroboration pattern: The Pragmatic Engineer independently documented the same failed requirements, so the video is accessible evidence of a claim already anchored at T1.

METR walked back confidence in its own method in 2026. Scandal or feature?

Feature. They named the mechanism (selection effects — developers refusing the no-AI arm), updated toward "newer tools likely help more," and left the public data trail intact. A system that dates its own corrections — like this book's DeepSeek pricing fix — deserves more trust, not less.

Beyond this build

Evaluating model comparisons. Vendor battle-cards, arena leaderboards, and "beats GPT-x" charts are claims — run the same checklist. Who submitted the prompts, does the eval predate training, is there a held-out split nobody could touch?

Reading vendor changelogs. Changelogs are T2: useful, timestamped, written by the interested party. Cross-check behavior changes against your own repro before promoting a changelog line to fact.

Applying tiers to any field. Nutrition studies, market research, product reviews — the coroner's questions are domain-neutral: funding, measurement, controls, contamination, scope, tier. Ten minutes per claim, and you stop laundering hype into knowledge, in code or anywhere else.
