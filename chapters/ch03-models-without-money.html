This is an on-ramp chapter, not an economics lesson — money appears here only where it blocks practice, and on day one it blocks almost everyone. Access is not one decision but four shapes, and every provider you will ever meet fits one of them:

  • Ride an entitlement you hold. A paid AI subscription whose terms allow harness reuse powers an agent at zero marginal cost.
  • Ride a published free tier. A vendor key with printed ceilings — requests per minute, requests per day.
  • Pay almost nothing per token. Cheap open-weight APIs price agent habits in pocket change, especially off-peak.
  • Run the weights yourself. Local models: no provider, no bill, no 429 — your hardware is the only wall.

The transferable skill is not memorizing pricing pages; it is reading a published limit before you build on it, and knowing which shape you're standing in. Every harness from Chapter 02 reaches all four through the same three config fields — endpoint, credential, model id — so switching doors later costs an evening, not a semester.

Door 1 · Reuse a subscription you already pay for

If any AI subscription touches your life, check whether its terms allow harness reuse before buying anything. opencode documents zero-setup OAuth for ChatGPT Plus/Pro, GitHub Copilot (device-flow), and GitLab Duo — sign in, and your existing plan becomes the model budget. Even a $0 ChatGPT plan draws from a shared Codex allowance pool, billed since April 2, 2026 in token-based credits. One boundary from Chapter 02: community proxies riding Claude Pro/Max subscriptions were removed in opencode v1.3.0 because Anthropic prohibits it — reuse doors exist where the vendor permits them, and vanish where it doesn't.

Door 2 · Published free tiers, read honestly

Gemini CLI's free tier remains the largest first-party allowance — 60 requests per minute, 1,000 per day on models with a 1M-token context window, unlocked by a personal Google OAuth click. OpenRouter's :free models publish their constants openly (20/min; 50 or 1,000/day by credit history). Groq's free plan is the instructive one: it caps both requests and tokens, at org level, and exempts cached tokens — a reward for prompt-cache discipline you'll meet again in Chapter 05. Two tiers stopped publishing grids entirely: Google's API-key free tier now shows live limits only inside AI Studio, Mistral's only in your console — so the modern ritual is "open the console, screenshot YOUR numbers, date them." And Qwen's new-user quota (~1M tokens per model, 90 days) can be locked in "Free Quota Only" mode, which hard-stops with HTTP 403 — a door that makes surprise bills structurally impossible.

Door 3 · Cheap open-weight APIs and the off-peak trick

Past the truly free doors sits a band so cheap that a month of daily practice rounds to pocket change. DeepSeek's V4-Flash runs $0.22/$0.66 per million input/output tokens — halved outside peak hours (everything except 01–04 and 06–10 UTC weekdays), with cache-hit input at $0.007/M. Do the month math once: 8M tokens in, 2M out costs $3.08 at list, roughly $1.54 shifted off-peak, less still as cache hits accumulate. Kimi prices K2.7 Code/K2.6 at $0.95/$4.00 per M with higher limits unlocking automatically as cumulative spend grows. DeepSeek even exposes an Anthropic-format endpoint, so Anthropic-flavored harnesses connect without translation. "Paid" here doesn't mean expensive; it means the meter exists.

Door 4 · Fully local: the wall moves in-house

Ollama and LM Studio run open weights on your own machine — LM Studio documents a provider at 127.0.0.1:1234/v1, and ollama launch opencode configures opencode automatically. Nothing leaves your machine; no rate limit can exist because there is nobody to enforce one. The honest costs: your RAM/VRAM sets the ceiling, and agent-grade work wants models with at least a 64k-token context window — smaller-context models starve a loop holding files, diffs, and tool output at once. Local is also the only door that survives airplanes.

§3

An honesty break: cheaper is not identical

Open-weight models are closer to frontier quality than ever — and the price ladders admit they are not identical. Kimi charges $3/$15 for its K3 flagship versus $0.95/$4.00 for its K2 code models; DeepSeek's V4-Pro costs three times its V4-Flash. Vendors price capability differences; pretend otherwise and you'll rediscover them mid-project. Practitioner stance: treat cheap-door output as draft-until-proven — keep the test-and-read-the-diff rituals doubled, not relaxed, while you evaluate whether a door holds for your work. When it holds, practice reps cost pennies. When it doesn't, your verification steps will catch it before a blog would have.

grey zone · risk-flagged

Some free doors train on what you send through them — documented, not rumor, and the paper trails differ in quality. OpenRouter's written policy (T1): providers flag per-endpoint whether they may train on your prompts, with separate opt-outs for paid and free models — and opting out can route you away from the cheapest providers. Contrast z.ai's GLM Coding Plan: community documentation (T3, community-reported — a lead, not settled fact) describes terms permitting training on your data with no meaningful opt-out, plus automated detection that throttles, suspends, then bans non-coding use at three violations; z.ai's own pages offer no written opt-out to point at. Working rule until you've read a provider's policy yourself: private code goes through written-opt-out doors or local weights.

Try it yourself — one door, end to end, tonight

  1. Exact-click checkpoint: open the recommended door. Go to https://github.com/google-gemini/gemini-cli, copy the install command for your OS from its README, paste it into a terminal, and run the launcher (gemini). When it offers auth, choose sign-in with Google and approve the personal-account OAuth screen — that approval is the moment the 60/min · 1,000/day tier attaches.   2. Run one real session, then probe the wall. In a small project folder, give one bounded goal ("explain what this folder contains"), accept and deny one permission each so you feel the gate. Then fire a quick burst of trivial prompts and watch for any rate-limit message — note its exact wording and reset hint. Compare what bit (minute wall vs day wall) against the picker above.

Self-check

Your agent dies mid-afternoon with HTTP 429. Which ceilings might you have hit, and how do you tell them apart?

At least three candidates: a daily request cap (resets on the provider's midnight), a per-minute request cap (clears in seconds — retry and it works), or a token-per-minute wall like Groq's 8K TPM (big contexts trip it at low request counts). Providers return a retry-after header on 429; its magnitude names the wall. Cached tokens being exempt is the tell of a token-bucket door.

Why is OpenRouter's $10-credit rule not really "paying for the model"?

The $10 is a one-time, refundable-in-practice trust deposit that flips the daily wall from 50 to 1,000 requests; the :free models themselves stay $0 per request. You paid to raise a limit, not to buy tokens — which is why the picker shows it as the same door with the marker slid right.

Name one situation where the local door wins outright despite weaker model quality — and its price.

Air-gapped or confidential code, offline work, or unlimited destructive experimentation where there is no shared pool to exhaust. The price: your hardware sets throughput and context size, and agent-grade local work demands ≥64k-token-context models or the loop starves.

Beyond this build

What changes when you eventually pay. The mental model doesn't — walls just get taller and start carrying invoices. Paid access swaps request-count walls for token buckets (Anthropic documents per-model RPM plus input/output token-per-minute ceilings, cached reads exempt) and monthly spend caps ($500 Start / $1k Build / $200k Scale). Vendor-reported averages — OpenAI cites roughly $100–200 per developer-month for Codex — deserve the same scrutiny as any vendor number. The wall-reading habit is identical; only the currency changed.

Team and organizational doors. Caps quietly become org-level the moment a second person shares your key: Groq's free tables are org-wide, Qwen's quota pools across users, and OpenRouter blocks the multi-account workaround explicitly. Teams need someone who owns keys, budgets, and the rate-limit doc — Build 3 (free-tier-atlas) turns exactly that skill into software.

Where local wins outright. Compliance-bound repositories where nothing may leave the building, offline environments, and workflows hammering thousands of trial iterations a day — no shared pool exists to exhaust. Everywhere else the calculus is honest: you trade convenience and peak quality for sovereignty and a $0 meter that can never surprise you.
